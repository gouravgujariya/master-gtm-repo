# Braun Copywriting — Poke the Bear Cold Email Skill

Principle-driven cold email writing based on Josh Braun's Poke the Bear methodology. This skill produces emails built from research, not templates. Every element earns its place through a specific principle.

## When to Use

- Writing cold email copy from market research, buyer voice data, or prospect intel
- Building poke-the-bear question matrices (for prospect intel reports, playbooks, or campaign planning)
- Crafting sample outbound emails written AS a prospect to their ICPs
- Reviewing existing email copy against Braun principles
- Need the "why" behind good cold email, not just the "how"

## When NOT to Use

- Operational campaign formatting, variable schemas, spintax, or ESP upload
- Writing at scale with templated variable fills
- Social media, newsletter, or content marketing copy


---

## The Core Principle

> **Your prospect is more interesting than your product.**

Prospects are hardwired to dance with the devil they know. Status quo bias. Change is risky. Your job is to shine a light on a big, expensive, unexamined problem so the prospect realizes the cost of doing nothing is greater than the risk of change.

**Different intent produces different behavior produces different results.**

- Salesperson intent: get the meeting, get the sale, close the deal → prospects feel the push → they pull away
- Braun intent: reach out to people who might have a problem you can solve. Some will want to talk. Some won't. Either way is fine. → prospects feel curiosity, not pressure → they engage

## The 6-Element Email Structure

Every Braun email contains up to 6 elements. Not all are required in every email, but the structure is the scaffold.

| # | Element | Purpose | How to Build It |
|---|---------|---------|----------------|
| 1 | **TRUTH** | Something specific you noticed about the prospect that ladders back to how you help | LinkedIn profile, website, recent post, podcast appearance, hiring page. Must be genuinely personal and specific. Not "I see you're in SaaS." |
| 2 | **THINK** | An illumination question that makes them scratch their head | See Illumination Question Construction below. The goal: "Hmm, I'm not sure." |
| 3 | **Cost of Inaction** | What it costs to do nothing | Data point, industry stat, or calculated cost. Must be sourced. Creates urgency without being pushy. |
| 4 | **Third Party Validation** | What similar companies are doing | Customer success story with specific numbers. The bandwagon effect. "ACME is doing X and getting Y." **Guardrail:** The sender cannot cite their own site or materials as third-party evidence. If Company X is sending the email, "testimonial on companyX.com" is their own marketing, not independent proof. Use the customer's name and metric directly, or cite an independent source (analyst report, press, G2 review). |
| 5 | **Curiosity Pique** | Hint at HOW without explaining | One phrase that makes the brain think "How do they do that? What do they know that I don't?" |
| 6 | **Low-Friction CTA** | Gauge interest, don't demand time | Binary question they can answer in 5 words or less. See CTA Patterns below. |

### Example (Annotated)

```
Hey John - Scanned your LinkedIn and noticed you built the outbound sales
process at Matillion in a few months. (TRUTH)

Curious, how do you know good interviews won't turn into bad sales hires?
(THINK - illumination question)

Average annual rep turnover rates are 24 to 30%, costing between 50-75%
of their annual salary (HBR). (Cost of Inaction)

ACME is using an unusual but effective approach to prevent bad sales hires.
Turnover rates under 5%. (Third Party Validation)

It involves hiring for the task. (Curiosity Pique)

Open to learning how they're doing it? Ben Jones. (Low-Friction CTA)
```

## Illumination Question Construction

The heart of Braun. An illumination question surfaces a problem the prospect hasn't consciously quantified.

### The Generative Question

Ask yourself: **"What do you know that your prospect might not know that can hurt them?"**

This is where upstream research is critical. Without deep understanding of the prospect's market (from market research, voice-of-customer data, or manual research), you're guessing. Bad illumination questions are just leading questions with better grammar.

### Primary Frames (Use These First)

1. **"How do you know..."** — Forces them to confront uncertainty
   - "How do you know the founder you're about to invest in won't have a sordid history splashed across TechCrunch?"
   - "How do you know good interviews won't turn into bad sales hires?"
   - "How do you know you're not being overcharged for shipping?"

2. **"What are you doing to ensure..."** — Implies a gap in their process
   - "What are you doing to ensure your wash mitt won't scratch your car?"
   - "What are you doing to ensure violations aren't occurring between quarterly pulls?"

### Secondary Frames (Use for CTAs and Follow-ups)

3. **"What if..."** — Contrasts what is with what's possible. The word "without" shows the contrast.
   - "What if you could have 15 conversations for every 50 outbound dials without adding anything to your tech stack?"
   - "What if you could recover 56% of failed payments without using internal resources?"

4. **"If you could... what impact would that have on..."** — Future pacing. Makes them imagine a better tomorrow.
   - "If you could calculate and run monthly payouts for 100+ reps in 5 minutes instead of 5 hours, how significantly would that change your life for the better?"
   - "If you could reduce the risk of losing winnable deals, what impact would that have on quota attainment?"

### What Makes an Illumination Question Good

- **Neutral.** No desired response baked in. You genuinely don't know their answer.
- **Specific.** References their industry, their process, their risk. Not generic.
- **Uncomfortable.** If they know the answer and it's good, it's the wrong question. If they don't know, or they know and it's bad, it's the right question.
- **Not leading.** "Wouldn't you like more leads?" is leading. "How do you know your pipeline isn't leaking qualified prospects between stages?" is illumination.

### Building Questions from Research Data

| Research Source | Question Pattern |
|----------------|-----------------|
| EDP from playbook | "How do you know [EDP consequence] isn't happening to you?" |
| Buyer voice quotes | "What are you doing about [exact pain language buyers used]?" |
| Competitive intel | "How do you know [competitor advantage] isn't costing you deals?" |
| Digital gaps | "When was the last time you checked [gap area] and liked what you saw?" |
| ICP pain stacks | "What's your process for [pain point] without [common workaround]?" |

### Building Questions from CSV Lead Data

When working with enriched lead lists (from lead enrichment data), map lead attributes to question angles:

| CSV Signal | Question Angle |
|-----------|---------------|
| Recently funded | "How do you know your [process] will scale with the team you're about to hire?" |
| High employee growth | "What are you doing to ensure new hires ramp without [specific risk]?" |
| Uses competitor tool | "How do you know [competitor tool] isn't [specific limitation]?" |
| Long tenure in role | "How do you know [process] hasn't drifted from best practice since you set it up?" |
| Hiring for specific role | "What are you doing to ensure your new [role] has [what they need] from day one?" |
| Tech stack signal | "How do you know [tool] and [tool] are actually talking to each other?" |

This is where Braun principles meet data at scale. The question is still principle-driven (not templated), but the ANGLE is informed by what you know about the individual lead.

## Crispy Emails

Your cold email competes against 304 emails a week, Slack, Netflix, TikTok, and checking phones 150 times per day. It must be crispy.

### What "Crispy" Means

1. Shines a light on a **big expensive problem** the prospect doesn't know they have
2. Shows **deep or specific expertise** about the problem
3. Communicates what's **meaningfully different**
4. Is **clear**
5. Shows the **contrast between before and after**
6. **Five lines or less**

### The Calorie Rule

Imagine each word has calories. If your email has too many calories, prospects can't digest it. Every word must earn its place. If removing a word doesn't change the meaning, remove it.

### Cutting Process

After drafting, run three passes:
1. **Fluff pass:** Remove anything that doesn't advance the message
2. **Compression pass:** Combine sentences, remove redundant phrases
3. **Adjective pass:** Kill adjectives and adverbs. "Highly innovative approach" becomes "new approach"

## Follow-Up Philosophy: One Chess Move Per Email

**Never bump things to the top of inboxes.** When you bump, you remind people why they didn't respond.

Instead: each follow-up email makes ONE chess move. One new problem you solve. One new angle.

### The Chess Move Pattern

Identify 3-4 different problems you solve for the prospect. Each becomes its own follow-up:

**Example for a triathlon coach:**
1. Email 1: Blowing up on the run
2. Email 2: Self-defeating mindset
3. Email 3: Bonking due to improper nutrition
4. Email 4: Fear of the swim

Each email is self-contained. Each surfaces a different pain. The prospect might not care about problem 1 but problem 3 hits home.

### Mapping Chess Moves from Research

| Research Source | Chess Moves |
|----------------|-------------|
| Pain stacks (from research) | Each pain = one chess move |
| Segment scoring (from research) | Each segment's top pain = one chess move |
| Buyer voice quotes (from playbook) | Each distinct complaint theme = one chess move |
| Campaign performance data (from campaign analytics) | Winning angles from past campaigns = validated chess moves |

## 4 Phrases to Never Use

1. **"Hello, my name is..."** — Social loafing research: people respond when they feel uniquely qualified. Start with what prompted you to contact THIS person. Move your name to the end.

2. **"Hope you're doing well"** — Not authentic. Generic. Cut it.

3. **"I know you're busy, I'll keep this brief"** — Ironic. Be brief instead of announcing it.

4. **"Just bumping this to the top of your inbox"** — Reminds them why they didn't reply. Use a chess move instead.

## Door Openers vs Door Slammers

**Door slammers** make prospects defensive:
- "I'd love to schedule a 30-minute call" (commission breath)
- "I would love to show you our solution" (your product, not their problem)

**Door openers** lower defenses:
- "Would it make sense to review what your options are for [their fear]?"
- "Would it make sense to..." leaves control with the prospect
- "Should a need arise in the future" reduces buying pressure
- "Review what your options are" signals no assumptions

### The Commission Breath Test

If the sentence starts with "I would love to," you have commission breath. You're more absorbed in your product than your prospect. Rewrite from their perspective.

## CTA Patterns (Low Friction Only)

Every CTA must pass the **5-word reply test**: can they respond in 5 words or less?

| Pattern | Example | When to Use |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| **Gauge interest** | "Open to learning how they're doing it?" | Default. Works everywhere. |
| **Worth exploring** | "Worth a brief email exchange?" | When you need permission to share more. |
| **Future pacing** | "If you could [outcome], what impact would that have?" | CTAs and follow-ups. Makes them imagine the better state. |
| **Would it make sense** | "Would it make sense to review your options for [area]?" | When the prospect needs to feel in control. |
| **No rush** | "No rush but is this worth exploring?" | Reduces pressure further. |
| **Resource offer** | "Want me to send over [specific deliverable]?" | When you have a lead magnet. |

**Never use:**
- "Can we schedule 15 minutes?"
- "Are you available for a quick call?"
- "Let's chat sometime"
- "Would love to connect"

## The Step-by-Step Email Building Process

This is the workflow for writing a single Braun email from scratch.

### Step 1: Find a Customer Quote (Third Party Validation)

Go to the company's website (or the prospect's competitor's website) and find:
- Case studies / success stories
- Before and after: what was the situation before, what happened after
- Specific numbers (time saved, money made, percentage improved)

**Variables to extract:**
- Before state (the pain, in the customer's words)
- After state (the result, with numbers)
- Cost of inaction (what happens if they don't change)

### Step 2: Find a Similar Prospect

Pick someone at a company similar to the case study customer. Then:
- Browse their LinkedIn profile for something personal and unique
- Look for: About section, recent posts, career transitions, shared interests, unusual background

### Step 3: Draw the Connection

Find a creative link between their personal detail and the problem you solve. This is the TRUTH element. It must feel natural, not forced.

**Example:** Matt's LinkedIn says he used to be an attorney in California. Your product saves time on monthly payment processing. Connection: "Does paying publishers take longer than finishing the MBE section of the California Bar Exam?"

### Step 4: Write the Email

Assemble: TRUTH (personal hook) + case study proof (Third Party Validation) + Low-Friction CTA.

### Step 5: Cut

Run the three cutting passes (fluff, compression, adjectives). Target 5 lines or less.

## Building a Poke the Bear Question Matrix

Used in prospect intel reports (Section 10) and campaign planning.

### Structure

| Pain Category | Poke the Bear Question | Why It Hurts | Data Behind It |
|--------------|----------------------|-------------|---------------|
| [from research] | [illumination question] | [what it forces them to confront] | [sourced stat or finding] |

### Process

1. **Pull pain categories** from upstream research (EDP, buyer voice, competitive intel, digital gaps)
2. **For each pain category**, write one illumination question using "How do you know..." or "What are you doing to ensure..." frames
3. **Add "Why It Hurts"** — what specifically this forces the prospect to face
4. **Add "Data Behind It"** — the sourced stat that validates the question
5. **Generate 6-8 questions per prospect** minimum

### Quality Test

- Every question must be answerable but uncomfortable
- If they know the answer and it's good, it's the wrong question
- If they don't know the answer, or they know it and it's bad, it's the right question
- Every question must reference something specific to the prospect's market (no generic questions)

## The Referral Play

Standard referral requests are withdrawals. "Do you know anyone who..." feels like unleashing a salesperson.

### The Deposit Alternative

Give first. Share something valuable, then ask if they know someone who'd benefit from it.

> "I'm sharing a slideshow on three ways to increase cold email response rates. Do you know any Directors of Business Development or VPs of Sales that might benefit from this? If you don't feel comfortable name-dropping, no worries. You don't even need to reply to this email."

**Key:** Teaching forges connection. You're giving, not getting.

## Applying Braun Principles to Offer Design

When crafting offers, pressure-test them through the Braun lens:

1. **Does the offer illuminate a blind spot?** If the prospect already knows they have this problem and is actively solving it, you're not poking the bear. You're joining a crowded conversation.

2. **Can you write an illumination question about it?** If you can't form a "How do you know..." question that makes the prospect uncomfortable, the offer isn't sharp enough.

3. **Does the offer have a natural cost-of-inaction argument?** If doing nothing doesn't cost them anything, the offer lacks urgency.

4. **Can you find third-party validation?** If nobody else has solved this problem with numbers to prove it, the offer lacks proof.

5. **Is the CTA naturally low-friction?** If the only next step is a 30-minute demo, the offer needs a lighter entry point.

## Review Checklist (Braun Principle Adherence)

When reviewing copy against Braun principles:

- [ ] Opens with TRUTH (specific personal observation), not product pitch
- [ ] Contains an illumination question (not a leading question)
- [ ] Illumination question uses "How do you know..." or "What are you doing to ensure..." frame (or "What if..." for follow-ups)
- [ ] Includes cost of inaction with sourced data
- [ ] Third party validation uses specific numbers, not vague claims
- [ ] Curiosity pique hints at mechanism without explaining
- [ ] CTA is low-friction (can reply in 5 words or less)
- [ ] No banned phrases ("My name is...", "Hope you're doing well", "I know you're busy", "Bumping this")
- [ ] No "I would love to..." (commission breath test)
- [ ] 5 lines or less (crispy)
- [ ] Every word has calories (no filler)
- [ ] Sentence rhythm varies (not all fragments, not all long - mix short punches with flowing sentences)
- [ ] Follow-ups use chess moves (new angle per email), not bumps
- [ ] Follow-up openers use natural registers ("forgot to mention", "had an idea"), not template phrases ("different angle entirely", "last thought on this")
- [ ] If this is a revision: does current version sound better or worse than previous when read fresh?
- [ ] Name at the end, not the beginning
